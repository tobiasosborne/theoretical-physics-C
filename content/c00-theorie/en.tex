\chapter{What a physical theory supplies}\label{c00}

This chapter says two things. First: what this course means by a physical theory, and in
which order it treats its theories. Second: on which findings classical physics failed, so
that a new theory was needed. There is little calculation here. The mathematics begins in
Chapter~1 with probability.

\section{Every experiment in three steps}\label{c00.s2}

The lecture course from which this course grew describes experiments with a single
scheme \srcref{tpc-script-v0.2}{lec01, box “Every experiment in three steps”}:
\begin{equation}\label{c00.eq.drei-stadien}\claim{c00.cl.drei-stadien}{source-quoted}
  \begin{aligned}
    &\text{\tpcifenglish{To the theorist all experiments in physics}{Für den Theoretiker sehen alle Experimente}} \\
    &\text{\tpcifenglish{look the same; they consist of three stages.}{der Physik gleich aus; sie bestehen aus drei Stadien.}}
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Preparation.} Something is set up: a die, or in the laboratory a beam of
    light, say.
  \item \emph{Run.} The experiment runs.
  \item \emph{Measurement.} At the end we look and obtain one of the possible
    outcomes.
\end{enumerate}
Figure~\ref{c00.fig.experiment} shows the scheme. An experimental physicist would not
accept this simplification; seen abstractly, though, every experiment looks like this.

\begin{tpcfigure}{c00.fig.experiment}
  \caption{The three stages of an experiment. The bracket combines the first two into a
    single preparation. The preparation is drawn solid, the run with a heavier line, the
    measurement dashed.}
\end{tpcfigure}

The first two steps can be combined into \emph{one} more complicated preparation. A loaded
die is nothing but a normal die to which something has been done beforehand. That is why
two notions often suffice: preparation and measurement. The chapter author places these
stages alongside the ingredients of sentence~\eqref{c00.eq.liefert}: preparation,
dynamics, measurement. This correspondence awaits the author's decision (G-0023).

% chapter author's (G-0023).

\tpcdef{D1}

The following explanation is the chapter author's reading and awaits the author's decision
(G-0023). A preparation is first of all something one \emph{does}: a list of instructions.
For the predictions, what counts is which probabilities the subsequent yes-no measurements
give. Two procedures that give the same probabilities for all yes-no questions are
described by the theory through the same state. The yes-no questions of
which the definition speaks are the simplest measurements: those with only two possible
outcomes. In Chapter~1 they are the indicator functions, and Chapter~2 defines them in
general.

\section{Why a new theory was needed}\label{c00.s4}

The lectures begin with a statement: we find ourselves in a quantum universe. Until 1900
its quantum aspects went unnoticed, because classical physics supplies a very good
approximation to the physics of everyday life. What needs explaining is therefore not
quantum mechanics but the quality of the classical approximation. The keyword for this is
\emph{decoherence}; it belongs to advanced quantum mechanics and is not treated here
\srcref{tpc-script-v0.2}{lec01, section 1}. The course works with the assumption
\begin{equation}\label{c00.eq.annahme}\claim{c00.cl.annahme}{source-quoted}
  \begin{aligned}
    &\text{\tpcifenglish{Our assumption is that quantum mechanics}{Unsere Annahme ist, dass die Quantenmechanik}} \\
    &\text{\tpcifenglish{is the correct description of the universe.}{die korrekte Beschreibung des Universums ist.}}
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

On method: one can teach quantum mechanics by first working through the wrong, classical
or semiclassical models and then showing that they do not work. The course does not take
that route. The Bohr model of the atom is worked through nowhere. The findings on which
classical physics failed are named nonetheless: two of them with their formula, the others
as a list. None is derived in this chapter. All statements of this section about
experiments come from the lecture notes and have not been checked here against a further
source.

\subsection{Black-body radiation}\label{c00.s4.1}

The lecture notes describe a black body as a body that reflects no incident radiation
and nevertheless radiates at a temperature $\Temp$ because of the thermally excited atoms.
As the usual realisation they name a box with a small hole from which black-body radiation
emerges (Figure~\ref{c00.fig.hohlraum}). This description is a statement of the lectures;
the physical justification of the cavity idealisation awaits a source or wording from
the author (G-0024).

\begin{tpcfigure}{c00.fig.hohlraum}
  \caption{The realisation of a black body named in the lecture notes: a box with a small
    hole. The drawn reflections do not hit the hole; the first six reflections of a ray
    according to the law of reflection are shown. The lecture notes call the radiation
    emerging from the hole black-body radiation. The drawing shows the ray path; the
    physical justification of the idealisation remains open (G-0024).}
\end{tpcfigure}

The spectrum of this radiation cannot be explained with classical physics. The right
spectrum follows from the assumption that light of frequency $\nu$ consists of light
quanta of energy
\begin{equation}\label{c00.eq.lichtquant}\claim{c00.cl.lichtquant}{source-backed}
  E = \hPlanck \nu
\end{equation}
each. The constant $\hPlanck$ is called the Planck constant. The radiation law itself
states how much energy per volume falls on the frequencies between $\nu$ and
$\nu + \dd\nu$:
\begin{equation}\label{c00.eq.planck}\claim{c00.cl.planck}{source-backed}
  u(\nu, \Temp)\,\dd\nu
  = \frac{8\pi \hPlanck \nu^3}{\clight^3}\,\frac{\dd\nu}{e^{\hPlanck\nu/(\kB \Temp)} - 1} .
\end{equation}
Here $\clight$ is the speed of light and $\kB$ the Boltzmann constant. The course derives
this law itself in the part on statistical physics, from the assumption that light is
quantised. Here it is only used.

In theoretical physics one mostly uses, instead of $\hPlanck$, the \emph{reduced Planck
constant}
\begin{equation}\label{c00.eq.hbar}\claim{c00.cl.hbar}{source-backed}
  \hbar = \frac{\hPlanck}{2\pi} .
\end{equation}
The numerical values are
$\hPlanck = \num[c00.cl.h-wert]{6.62607015 \cdot 10^{-34}}{J\,s}$ and
$\hbar = \num[c00.cl.hbar-wert]{1.054571817 \cdot 10^{-34}}{J\,s}$; the second is
truncated to ten digits.

For $\nu > 0$ and $\Temp > 0$ we consider the exponent
$x = \hPlanck\nu/(\kB \Temp)$. For small $x$,
$e^x - 1$ is nearly equal to $x$. Inserting this into \eqref{c00.eq.planck}, $\hPlanck$
cancels, and what remains is the function
\begin{equation}\label{c00.eq.grenzform}\claim{c00.cl.grenzform}{derived}
  u_{\mathrm{kl}}(\nu, \Temp) = \frac{8\pi \nu^2 \kB \Temp}{\clight^3},
  \qquad
  \frac{u(\nu, \Temp)}{u_{\mathrm{kl}}(\nu, \Temp)} = \frac{x}{e^{x} - 1}
  \quad \text{\tpcifenglish{with}{mit}}\ x = \frac{\hPlanck\nu}{\kB \Temp} .
\end{equation}
The second equation holds exactly for $\nu > 0$ and $\Temp > 0$; one obtains it by dividing
\eqref{c00.eq.planck} by $u_{\mathrm{kl}}$. The ratio $x/(e^x - 1)$ is close to $1$ for
small $x$ and falls to $0$ for large $x$\zoomref{c00.v1}. The function $u_{\mathrm{kl}}$ no longer contains $\hPlanck$ and is here the algebraically
obtained limiting form of the radiation law given in the lecture notes
\srcref{tpc-script-v0.2}{lec01, prehistory, item 1}.

\begin{tpcfigure}{c00.fig.planck}
  \caption{The radiation law~\eqref{c00.eq.planck} and its limiting form
    \eqref{c00.eq.grenzform}. (a) The energy density $u$ for three temperatures $T_0$,
    $\tfrac56 T_0$ and $\tfrac23 T_0$ (solid, dashed, dash-dotted), plotted against
    $\hPlanck\nu/(\kB T_0)$ in units of
    $u_0 = 8\pi(\kB T_0)^3/(\hPlanck^2\clight^3)$. The thin dashed parabola is
    the algebraic limiting form $u_{\mathrm{kl}}$ for the highest temperature $T_0$.
    At small positive frequencies its ratio to the solid theoretical curve is close to
    $1$; the parabola then runs upwards out of the picture.
    (b) The ratio $u/u_{\mathrm{kl}} = x/(e^x-1)$ for $\nu > 0$ and $\Temp > 0$.
    It depends only on
    $x = \hPlanck\nu/(\kB\Temp)$. The hatched area is the distance from the limiting
    form. It becomes noticeable as soon as $\hPlanck\nu$ reaches the size of $\kB\Temp$.}
\end{tpcfigure}

Figure~\ref{c00.fig.planck} shows both. The limiting form grows like $\nu^2$ without end,
whereas the curves of the radiation law have a maximum and fall off after it. The
difference becomes visible as soon as the energy $\hPlanck\nu$ of a light quantum is no
longer small compared with $\kB\Temp$.

\begin{vertiefung}[id=c00.v1,
    titel={The limiting form, taken precisely},
    wozu={The Core says that the ratio $x/(e^x-1)$ is close to $1$ for small $x$ and falls
      to $0$ for large $x$. Here are two bounds that prove it, and a consequence: the
      limiting form predicts an infinite energy density, the radiation law a finite one.},
    voraussetzung={Core up to equation~\eqref{c00.eq.grenzform}; the exponential series
      and integrals of powers. The rest of the chapter does not need this block.},
    zurueck={For $\hPlanck\nu$ small compared with $\kB\Temp$ the radiation law and the
      limiting form agree; for large frequencies the limiting form is wrong, and
      arbitrarily wrong.}]
  For $x > 0$, $e^x = 1 + x + \tfrac12 x^2 + \tfrac16 x^3 + \dots$ is a sum of positive
  terms. Two estimates for $e^x - 1$ follow. Dropping all terms from the third on makes
  the sum smaller: $e^x - 1 \geq x + \tfrac12 x^2$. Factoring out $x$ instead leaves
  $1 + \tfrac12 x + \tfrac16 x^2 + \dots$ in the bracket, and each of its terms is at most
  as large as the corresponding term $1 + x + \tfrac12 x^2 + \dots$ of the series for
  $e^x$: hence $e^x - 1 \leq x\,e^x$. Together this gives, for all $x > 0$,
  \begin{equation}\label{c00.eq.schranken}\claim{c00.cl.schranken}{derived}
    e^{-x} \;\leq\; \frac{x}{e^{x} - 1} \;\leq\; \frac{1}{1 + \tfrac12 x} .
  \end{equation}
  For $x \to 0$ both bounds tend to $1$, for $x \to \infty$ both tend to $0$. At
  $x = 1$, that is $\hPlanck\nu = \kB\Temp$, the ratio lies between $1/e$ and
  $\tfrac23$.

  Now to the total energy density, the integral over all frequencies. For the limiting
  form,
  \begin{equation}\label{c00.eq.divergenz}\claim{c00.cl.divergenz}{derived}
    \int_0^{\nu_1} u_{\mathrm{kl}}(\nu, \Temp)\,\dd\nu
    = \frac{8\pi \kB \Temp}{3\,\clight^3}\,\nu_1^3 ,
  \end{equation}
  and this grows beyond every bound with the upper limit $\nu_1$. The limiting form
  predicts an infinite energy density for every temperature $\Temp > 0$.

  For the radiation law the substitution $\nu = x\,\kB\Temp/\hPlanck$ leads to
  \begin{equation}\label{c00.eq.gesamt}\claim{c00.cl.gesamt}{derived}
    \begin{aligned}
      \int_0^{\infty} u(\nu, \Temp)\,\dd\nu
      &= \frac{8\pi (\kB\Temp)^4}{\hPlanck^3 \clight^3} \int_0^\infty \frac{x^3}{e^x - 1}\,\dd x , \\
      \int_0^\infty \frac{x^3}{e^x - 1}\,\dd x &\leq \frac13 + 120 .
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation}
  The bound for the integral is obtained in two pieces. For $0 < x \leq 1$,
  \eqref{c00.eq.schranken} gives $x^3/(e^x-1) \leq x^2$, and
  $\int_0^1 x^2\,\dd x = \tfrac13$. For $x \geq 1$ one uses the term $x^5/120$ of the
  exponential series: $e^x - 1 \geq x^5/120$, hence $x^3/(e^x-1) \leq 120/x^2$, and
  $\int_1^\infty 120\,x^{-2}\,\dd x = 120$. The integral is a pure number. The total
  energy density of the radiation law is therefore finite and proportional to $\Temp^4$.
  The course needs the value of the number only in the part on statistical physics.
\end{vertiefung}

\subsection{Photoelectric effect}\label{c00.s4.2}

According to the lecture notes, light can release electrons. For the energy of these
photoelectrons the lectures give the relation:
\begin{equation}\label{c00.eq.photoeffekt}\claim{c00.cl.photoeffekt}{source-backed}
  E_{\mathrm{el}} = \tfrac12 \massE v^2 = \hPlanck\nu - W .
\end{equation}
Here $\massE$ is the mass and $v$ the speed of the electron, and $W$ is its binding
energy in the lecture notes. The regime of this relation awaits a source or wording
from the author (G-0024). The lectures interpret the observation as an indication of
light quanta of energy~\eqref{c00.eq.lichtquant}
\srcref{tpc-script-v0.2}{lec01, prehistory, item 2}.

A kinetic energy is never negative. Equation~\eqref{c00.eq.photoeffekt} can therefore
hold only if
\begin{equation}\label{c00.eq.schwelle}\claim{c00.cl.schwelle}{derived}
  \hPlanck\nu \geq W,
  \qquad \text{\tpcifenglish{that is}{das heißt}} \qquad
  \nu \geq \frac{W}{\hPlanck} .
\end{equation}
For fixed $W$ the given relation describes $E_{\mathrm{el}}$ as a straight line of
slope $\hPlanck$
that begins at $\nu = W/\hPlanck$ (Figure~\ref{c00.fig.photoeffekt}). The slope is the
same for every value of $W$.

\begin{tpcfigure}{c00.fig.photoeffekt}
  \caption{The relation~\eqref{c00.eq.photoeffekt} given in the lecture notes, plotted
    algebraically for two fixed values of the binding energy, $W_1$
    (solid) and $W_2 = \tfrac85 W_1$ (dashed; the ratio is chosen for the picture). Both
    lines have the same slope $\hPlanck$: to a difference $\nu' - \nu$ in frequency belongs
    the difference $\hPlanck(\nu' - \nu)$ in energy. Each line begins at its threshold

\end{tpcfigure}

\subsection{Further findings}\label{c00.s4.3}

The lectures name four further findings \srcref{tpc-script-v0.2}{lec01, prehistory, items 3 to 6}.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Stability of atoms, line spectra, Franck-Hertz experiment.} According to the
    lecture notes, electron orbits in classical physics would be unstable: by the Liénard-Wiechert
    potentials of electrodynamics every accelerated point charge radiates. An electron on
    an orbit around the nucleus would lose energy all the time and would have to fall into
    the nucleus, which it does not. The lecture notes interpret line spectra and
    Franck-Hertz experiments as an indication of certain discrete energy levels in atoms.
  \item \emph{Compton effect.} The lectures treat the scattering of light off electrons
    with light quanta of energy~\eqref{c00.eq.lichtquant} and momentum $p$ of
    equation~\eqref{c00.eq.impuls}, using conservation of energy and momentum. Which part
    of the scattering prediction this covers awaits a source or wording from the author
    (G-0024).
  \item \emph{Stern-Gerlach experiment.} The lecture notes describe the experiment in terms
    of finitely many orientations of angular momentum relative to an applied magnetic
    field. The scope of this description awaits a source or wording from the author
    (G-0024).
  \item \emph{Interference of electrons.} The diffraction of electrons from crystals
    (Davisson-Germer experiment) is an indication of wave properties of the electron.
\end{enumerate}
The momentum of a light quantum of wavelength $\lambda$ is
\begin{equation}\label{c00.eq.impuls}\claim{c00.cl.impuls}{source-backed}
  p = \frac{\hPlanck}{\lambda} .
\end{equation}
The course will treat the line spectrum of hydrogen at length in Chapter~11. Years and
names belonging to these findings are not part of the Core\zoomref{c00.v2}.

  According to the lecture notes Planck originally introduced the constant $\hPlanck$ as an
  “auxiliary quantity”. In 1926 Schrödinger published a series of papers on wave
  mechanics. A parallel approach began in 1925 with reflections of Heisenberg; from it came
  the matrix mechanics of Born, Jordan and Heisenberg and a version by Dirac. Still in 1926
  Schrödinger showed the equivalence of the two approaches, and Born supplied the
  statistical interpretation of the theory. The author of the lectures regards Born's
  contribution as the most important aspect of the whole theory.

  Almost at the same time the theory took on the mathematical form it has today. Born took
  advice from Norbert Wiener on how to turn matrices into operators. Hilbert gave a
  lecture course on quantum mechanics in Göttingen in the winter semester 1926/27, and his
  assistant John von Neumann introduced Hilbert space, density matrices and the entropy of
  quantum states. The role of group theory was clarified by Wigner and Weyl. The
  interpretation of the theory remained disputed and still is; according to the lecture
  notes there is to this day no contradiction with quantum mechanics.

  \begin{historical}
    Whoever reads German is in luck: the original papers of Planck, Schrödinger, Heisenberg
    and Born are written in German.
  \end{historical}
\end{vertiefung}

\subsection{Two sentences of the lectures}\label{c00.s4.4}

The lectures sum up the findings in two sentences
\srcref{tpc-script-v0.2}{lec01, the two yellow boxes}. The first:
\begin{equation}\label{c00.eq.welle-teilchen}\claim{c00.cl.welle-teilchen}{source-quoted}
  \begin{aligned}
    &\text{\tpcifenglish{Matter and radiation possess}{Materie und Strahlung besitzen}} \\
    &\text{\tpcifenglish{both wave and particle properties.}{sowohl Wellen- als auch Teilcheneigenschaften.}}
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The second:
\begin{equation}\label{c00.eq.kernsatz}\claim{c00.cl.kernsatz}{source-quoted}
  \begin{aligned}
    &\text{\tpcifenglish{Quantum mechanics is a probabilistic theory.}{Die Quantenmechanik ist eine probabilistische Theorie.}} \\
    &\text{\tpcifenglish{It therefore gives us predictions}{Sie liefert uns somit nur Vorhersagen}} \\
    &\text{\tpcifenglish{only in the form of probabilities.}{in Form von Wahrscheinlichkeiten.}}
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The second sentence stands above the first. “Wave and particle properties” remains a
little vague; the probabilistic statement is precise and underlies every quantum
phenomenon. The lectures add: in every oral examination the question is asked what the
most important statement about quantum mechanics is.

\begin{merksatz}[Key statement]
  Quantum mechanics is a probabilistic theory: it predicts probabilities. That is
  sentence~\eqref{c00.eq.kernsatz} of the lectures. The chapter author proposes to connect
  it with P0 in sentence~\eqref{c00.eq.p0} for quantum mechanics; this reading awaits
  the author's decision (G-0023).
\end{merksatz}

The lecture notes set classical physics against this: there the predictions are definite,
the particle is at this place at this time. As a finite example for
