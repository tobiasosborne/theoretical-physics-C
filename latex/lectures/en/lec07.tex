\section{Lecture: ``Quantisation''}
The postulates of the last lecture leave a gap: they do not say which Hilbert space, which Hamiltonian and which observables belong to a given system. There is no systematic method for this. Given a classical description of a system, there is no well-defined map that assigns to it the correct quantum system.

The reason is \emph{decoherence}. Classical physics is a good approximation for quantum systems that interact with their surroundings, and closed systems hardly exist in practice: every charged object couples to the electromagnetic field, which is itself a quantum system. If we move such an object back and forth it radiates, and the radiation carries information away at the speed of light. One would now like to recover the original quantum system from the classical description alone, that is, to undo decoherence. This fictitious inversion procedure is called ``quantisation'', and in general it is impossible.

\begin{greybox}[An inverse problem]
	Decoherence maps different quantum systems onto the same classical system: two points land on one. Nor can a face be reconstructed from the noisy image of a surveillance camera.
\end{greybox}

We get around the difficulty by trial and error. Decades of experience have given us a list of Hilbert spaces, Hamiltonians and observables that we know to describe broad classes of systems correctly; all fundamental interactions except gravity have been understood in this way. In this lecture and the next we set up the most important entry on this list, the particle. Never think that there is a simple recipe: if it were that easy, we would not need experimental physics.

\subsection{The Position Basis}
So far the Hilbert space has been finite-dimensional. For a particle on the line it no longer is: $\Hil = L^2(\mathbb{R})$, the space of square-integrable functions $\Psi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}$,
\begin{equation}
	\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \abs{\Psi(x)}^2\,dx < \infty, \qquad \braket{\Phi}{\Psi} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \Phi^*(x)\Psi(x)\,dx.
\end{equation}
The ket $\ket{\Psi}$ stands for the function $\Psi$. Dirac, and most physicists with him, go one step further and write the value of the function at the point $x$ as a scalar product with an ``improper'' bra,
\begin{equation}
	\braket{x}{\Psi} = \Psi(x).
\end{equation}
Up to here everything was mathematically in order; from here on we deliberately leave safe ground. The bra $\bra{x}$ belongs to no vector of the Hilbert space. We nevertheless calculate with the kets $\ket{x} = (\bra{x})\adj$, $x\in\mathbb{R}$, as if they were an orthonormal basis, the \emph{position basis}, with one change: in place of the Kronecker delta comes Dirac's delta function,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:posbasis}
	\braket{x}{y} = \delta(x-y), \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(y)\,\delta(x-y)\,dy = f(x).
\end{equation}
The second equation is all we need of $\delta$: under an integral it sets $y = x$. A function with this property does not exist; for $y = x$ the right-hand side of the first equation is not one but $\delta(0)$, and such expressions must be avoided. Sums over the basis become integrals:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:posentwicklung}
	\ket{\Psi} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \Psi(x)\ket{x}\,dx, \qquad \mathbb{I} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \ketbra{x}{x}\,dx.
\end{equation}
The function $\Psi$ is thus the list of coefficients of $\ket{\Psi}$ in the position basis; it is called the \emph{wave function}. With Eq.~(\ref{eq:posbasis}) one calculates as in the finite-dimensional case, for example
\begin{equation}
	\braket{\Psi}{\Psi} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \Psi^*(x)\Psi(y)\braket{x}{y}\,dx\,dy = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \abs{\Psi(x)}^2\,dx.
\end{equation}

\subsection{A Particle in One Dimension}
The most important system that quantum mechanics describes successfully is the particle in one dimension. As always we dutifully work through the postulates.

\emph{P1 and P2.} The Hilbert space is $\Hil = L^2(\mathbb{R})$. For the time being we calculate only with pure states $\rho = \ketbra{\Psi}{\Psi}$; every density operator is a mixture of pure states, and for mixtures one averages the probabilities obtained in this way. Only normalised wave functions are allowed, $\braket{\Psi}{\Psi} = 1$. So far the wave function is therefore nothing but notation; it acquires meaning only through measurements.

\emph{P3.} Here we describe position detectors only. Such a detector monitors an interval $[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}$, $a<b$, and answers ``yes'' if it finds the particle there. Its operator is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ortsdetektor}
	\op{F}_{[a,b]} = \int_a^b \ketbra{x}{x}\,dx.
\end{equation}
These operators do not cover all physically possible detectors, but they are the basis for everything that follows. We are not tied to intervals: for disjoint regions $S_1$, $S_2$ we have $\op{F}_{S_1\cup S_2} = \op{F}_{S_1} + \op{F}_{S_2}$.

\emph{P4.} With what probability do we find the particle in the state $\ket{\Psi}$ between $a$ and $b$? The response frequency is $\tr(\rho\,\op{F}_{[a,b]}) = \melem{\Psi}{\op{F}_{[a,b]}}{\Psi}$, and with Eqs.~(\ref{eq:posentwicklung}) and (\ref{eq:posbasis}) this becomes
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		\mathbb{P}\bigl[x\in[a,b]\bigr] &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!dx\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!dy\int_a^b\!dz\;\Psi^*(x)\Psi(y)\braket{x}{z}\braket{z}{y} \\
		&= \int_a^b \abs{\Psi(z)}^2\,dz.
	\end{split}
\end{equation}
For $a\to-\infty$, $b\to\infty$ this is the normalisation, $\mathbb{P}[x\in\mathbb{R}] = 1$. Because $\abs{\Psi(x)}^2$ is non-negative,
\begin{equation}
	\eqbox{p(x) = \abs{\Psi(x)}^2}
\end{equation}
is a probability density, the density of the position measurement.

\begin{intuition}
	The wave function assigns a \emph{complex} number to every possible position and delivers the position only indirectly: the probability of finding the particle between $a$ and $b$ is the area under the curve $p(x)$. You should resist the temptation to picture $\Psi$ itself as a density.
\end{intuition}

Why an interval and not a point? The probability of finding the particle \emph{exactly} at the point $x$ is always zero. Accordingly the object $\ketbra{x}{x}$, taken on its own, is not an operator and certainly not a detector. One sees this from the projector property:
\begin{equation}
	\bigl(\ketbra{x}{x}\bigr)^2 = \ket{x}\braket{x}{x}\bra{x} = \delta(0)\,\ketbra{x}{x},
\end{equation}
and $\delta(0)$ is not a number. For an interval, by contrast, what sits in the middle is $\braket{x}{y} = \delta(x-y)$ under two integrals, and one of them is disposed of:
\begin{equation}
	\op{F}_{[a,b]}^2 = \int_a^b\!\!\int_a^b \ket{x}\braket{x}{y}\bra{y}\,dx\,dy = \int_a^b \ketbra{x}{x}\,dx = \op{F}_{[a,b]}.
\end{equation}
$\op{F}_{[a,b]}$ really is a projector, and hence a detector in the sense of P3.
\begin{merksatz}
	Stand-alone ket-bra combinations of the position basis are to be avoided; they are not operators. Under an integral the same expressions are harmless.
\end{merksatz}

\subsection{Position, Momentum and Energy}
Informally one may picture $\ketbra{x}{x}\,dx$ as the event that the particle is found in a strip of width $dx$ at $x$. The position measurement has real outcomes, and by Eq.~(\ref{eq:observableA}) it has the Hermitian operator
\begin{equation}
	\eqbox{\op{x} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x\,\ketbra{x}{x}\,dx,}
\end{equation}
the \emph{position operator}. It is the first moment of the position measurement; the hat is there to remind us that this is an operator and not a number. It acts as a multiplication operator, $\Psi(x)$ turns into $x\,\Psi(x)$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:xwirkung}
	\melem{x}{\op{x}}{\Psi} = x\,\Psi(x), \qquad \langle\op{x}\rangle = \tr(\rho\,\op{x}) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x\,\abs{\Psi(x)}^2\,dx = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x\,p(x)\,dx.
\end{equation}
The second equation holds in the pure state $\rho = \ketbra{\Psi}{\Psi}$ and is the mean position of the particle. Behind the symbol $\langle\op{x}\rangle$ there lies a whole procedure: prepare, measure, write down, many times over, until the average settles.

An eigenvector $\op{x}\ket{u} = \lambda\ket{u}$ would have a delta function as its wave function and would therefore be an element of the position basis. One nevertheless speaks of ``position eigenvectors'' all the time; they are simply idealised objects, not elements of $\Hil$. Physically this means that a particle cannot be prepared in the ``classical state'' $\ket{\Psi} = \ket{x}$; you would need a device of infinite precision. Real pure states are always superpositions over many positions.

The \emph{momentum operator} of the particle is
\begin{equation}
	\eqbox{\op{p} = -i\hbar\frac{d}{dx},} \qquad \text{that is} \qquad \melem{x}{\op{p}}{\Psi} = -i\hbar\frac{d}{dx}\Psi(x).
\end{equation}
Why? One can put forward many heuristic arguments for this choice, but the honest answer is that nature wanted it this way. $\op{p}$ cannot be applied to every element of $L^2(\mathbb{R})$; we calculate with smooth wave functions that fall off quickly at infinity. What we can check is that the choice is admissible: $\op{p}$ is Hermitian. For such wave functions integration by parts gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phermitesch}
	\melem{\Phi}{\op{p}}{\Psi} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \Phi^*(x)\Bigl(-i\hbar\frac{d\Psi}{dx}\Bigr)dx = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \Bigl(-i\hbar\frac{d\Phi}{dx}\Bigr)^{\!*}\Psi(x)\,dx.
\end{equation}
The sign from the integration by parts is absorbed by the $i$; without the $i$, $d/dx$ would be anti-Hermitian.

\emph{P5.} The Hamiltonian of the free particle of mass $m$ is the kinetic energy,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hfrei}
	\op{H} = \frac{\op{p}^2}{2m}, \qquad \melem{x}{\op{H}}{\Psi} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\Psi(x).
\end{equation}
Where does this expression come from? From the only quantisation rule we have: the Hamiltonian is the classical energy, with a hat on it. Classically $E = p^2/2m$; we put the hat on. This is a rule of thumb and not a procedure. Every quantisation remains a guess, which only experiment can decide.

\begin{example}[Equal superposition over an interval]
	Suppose a particle has been prepared in an equal superposition over $[-1,1]$:
	\begin{equation}
		\Psi(x) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{\sqrt2}, & x\in[-1,1], \\ 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}
	\end{equation}
	Before we calculate, let us guess: by symmetry the mean position should be zero. First the normalisation, then the first two moments:
	\begin{equation}
		\braket{\Psi}{\Psi} = \int_{-1}^{1}\frac12\,dx = 1, \quad \langle\op{x}\rangle = \int_{-1}^{1}\frac{x}{2}\,dx = 0, \quad \langle\op{x}^2\rangle = \int_{-1}^{1}\frac{x^2}{2}\,dx = \frac13.
	\end{equation}
	The position measurement therefore scatters about zero, with standard deviation $\sqrt{\langle\op{x}^2\rangle - \langle\op{x}\rangle^2} = 1/\sqrt3$. This example is no use for the momentum: $\Psi$ jumps at the edges and cannot be differentiated there.
\end{example}

	$\Psi(x) = (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/4}\,e^{-x^2/(4\sigma^2)}\,e^{ikx}$, with width $\sigma>0$ and wave number $k\in\mathbb{R}$. You will need the Gaussian integrals, for $\alpha>0$:
	\begin{equation}\label{eq:gaussintegrale}
		\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-\alpha x^2}\,dx = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\alpha}}, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x^2\,e^{-\alpha x^2}\,dx = \frac{1}{2\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\alpha}}.
	\end{equation}
\end{exercise}
