\section{Lecture: Hilbert Space}
The \emph{Hilbert space} will play a major role in describing the states of quantum mechanics. To define this mathematical space we first need \emph{complex vector spaces}. Such spaces are defined in complete analogy with the real case.

Why \emph{complex} and not real vectors appear here, we do not know: nature is simply built that way, and no better alternative has been found so far.

A \emph{complex vector} $\mbf{v}\in \mathbb{C}^n$ is a column of $n$ complex numbers
\begin{equation}
	\mbf{v} = 
	\begin{pmatrix}
		v_1 \\ v_2 \\ \vdots \\ v_n
	\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The following operations are defined
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Multiplication by a scalar $a\in\mathbb{C}$
	\begin{equation}
		a\mbf{v} = 
	\begin{pmatrix}
		av_1 \\ av_2 \\ \vdots \\ av_n
	\end{pmatrix}.
	\end{equation}
	\item Addition
	\begin{equation}
		\mbf{v}+\mbf{w} = \begin{pmatrix}
		v_1 \\ v_2 \\ \vdots \\ v_n
	\end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix}
		w_1 \\ w_2 \\ \vdots \\ w_n
	\end{pmatrix} =
	\begin{pmatrix}
		v_1+w_1 \\ v_2+w_2 \\ \vdots \\ v_n+w_n
	\end{pmatrix}.
	\end{equation}
	\item Dagger operation
	\begin{equation}
		v^\dag = \begin{pmatrix}
		v_1 \\ v_2 \\ \vdots \\ v_n
	\end{pmatrix}^\dag \equiv \begin{pmatrix}
		v_1^* & v_2^* & \cdots & v_n^*
	\end{pmatrix}
	\end{equation}
	\item Inner product
	\begin{equation}
		\mbf{v}^\dag\mbf{w} = \begin{pmatrix}
		v_1^* & v_2^* & \cdots & v_n^*
	\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}
		w_1 \\ w_2 \\ \vdots \\ w_n
	\end{pmatrix} =
	\sum_{j=1}^n v_j^*w_j.
	\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
The space $V$ of all such vectors with fixed $n$ is called a \emph{complex vector space}.

For the vector space itself the first two rules suffice; the dagger operation and the inner product are extra structure, and only with them does it become a Hilbert space.

Here $n$ is defined as the \emph{dimension} of $V$. The notation $\mathbb{C}^n$ is often used for the vector space $V$ defined above.

\begin{example}
	The vector space $\mathbb{C}^2$ consists of all two-dimensional vectors
	\begin{equation}
		\mbf{v} = 
		\begin{pmatrix}
			v_1 \\ v_2
		\end{pmatrix}.
	\end{equation}
	Let
	\begin{equation}
		\mbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix}
			1+i \\ 1-i
		\end{pmatrix} \quad \text{and} \quad \mbf{w} = \begin{pmatrix}
			1 \\ -1
		\end{pmatrix}
	\end{equation}
	be two vectors. Their inner product reads
	\begin{equation}
		\mbf{v}^\dag \mbf{w} = \begin{pmatrix}
			1-i & 1+i
		\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}
			1 \\ -1
		\end{pmatrix} = -2i.
	\end{equation}
\end{example}

Infinite-dimensional vector spaces are possible as well and play a very important role in the description of quantum mechanical systems. The reason is a physical one: our best models are fields, and fields have infinitely many degrees of freedom. How are such vector spaces to be understood? One can picture infinite-dimensional vectors as columns with infinitely many entries, e.g.:
\begin{equation}
	\mbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix}
		v_1 \\ v_2 \\ \vdots \\
	\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
All the rules of calculation for vector spaces hold in the infinite-dimensional case too. The inner product $\mbf{v}^\dag \mbf{w}$ is computed as follows
\begin{equation}
	\mbf{v}^\dag\mbf{w} =
	\sum_{j=1}^\infty v_j^*w_j.
\end{equation}
This means, as a rule, that one sets $n=\infty$ in all equations. There are several subtle points one would have to take into account in order to work rigorously with infinite-dimensional vector spaces. In this course, however, we shall only mention these points in passing.

One of them shows up straight away. The vector made up entirely of ones,
\begin{equation}
	\mbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix}
		1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \\ \vdots
	\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
is a perfectly legal infinite list, but its inner product with itself diverges:
\begin{equation}
	\mbf{v}^\dag\mbf{v} = \sum_{j=1}^\infty 1 = \infty.
\end{equation}
Physically the answer to this is simple --- we do not admit such vectors. A vector is allowed precisely when
\begin{equation}
	\eqbox{\mbf{v}^\dag\mbf{v} = \sum_{j=1}^\infty |v_j|^2 < \infty}
\end{equation}
holds. This set is again a complex vector space; it is denoted by $\ell^2$, where the two comes from the square in the sum. Nothing can then become infinitely large when we compute probabilities.

For us the most important example of an infinite-dimensional vector space is the space of \emph{square-integrable functions} mapping $\mathbb{R}$ into $\mathbb{C}$ (which is denoted by $L^{2}(\mathbb{R})$). Here square-integrable means that
\begin{equation}
	\int_{-\infty}^\infty |f(x)|^2 \, dx < \infty.
\end{equation}
Square-integrable functions can be added, subtracted, and multiplied by scalars. Under these operations the results always stay square-integrable. The set of all square-integrable functions therefore forms an abstract vector space. One regards a square-integrable function $f(x)$ as a vector. Here too there is an inner product; an integral takes the place of the sum:
\begin{equation}
	\langle f, g\rangle \equiv \int_{-\infty}^\infty f^*(x)\, g(x)\, dx.
\end{equation}

\begin{example}
	A \emph{non}-example of a square-integrable function is supplied by the constant function $f(x) = 1$:
	\begin{equation}
		\int_{-\infty}^\infty |f(x)|^2\, dx = \int_{-\infty}^\infty 1\, dx = \infty.
	\end{equation}
	This is the counterpart of the vector made up entirely of ones from before.
\end{example}

\begin{greybox}[Three spaces, three systems]
	With this we have essentially all the Hilbert spaces we shall need: $\mathbb{C}^2$ belongs to the \emph{qubit}, $\ell^2$ to the \emph{harmonic oscillator} (a photon in an optical fibre, say) and $L^2(\mathbb{R})$ to a \emph{particle in one dimension}.
\end{greybox}
