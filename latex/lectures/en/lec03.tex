\section{Lecture: Dirac Notation, Hilbert Space, Bases}

\subsection{Dirac notation}
Let $V$ be a complex vector space; for now, think of $\mathbb{C}^n$ from the last lecture. In quantum mechanics the following notation is used universally: from now on we write every vector $\vb{v}\in V$ as a \emph{ket} $\ket{v}$. We drop the bold face inside the ket; the bar and the angle bracket already say that a vector is meant. For now the notation means nothing more, and why we introduce it will become clear soon. The real hurdle at the beginning is not the physics, but the notation.

\begin{example}
	Let $V = \mathbb{C}^2$, the space with which we shall describe the states of a qubit. From the two vectors
	\begin{equation}
		\ket{e_0} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
		\ket{e_1} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}
	\end{equation}
	we form two new ones by adding and multiplying by numbers,
	\begin{equation}
		\ket{e_\pm} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{e_0} \pm \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{e_1}
		= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ \pm 1 \end{pmatrix}.
	\end{equation}
	The vectors $\ket{e_\pm}$ keep turning up, in angular momentum, in the hydrogen atom and especially in spin systems.
\end{example}

Here $\ket{v + w}$ stands for $\ket{v} + \ket{w}$, and $\ket{zv}$ for $z\ket{v}$ when $z$ is a number.

The ket notation is complemented by the \emph{bra} vectors. With the inner product $\vb{v}\adj\vb{w}$ the bra $\bra{v}$ is the conjugated row that the dagger operation makes from the column $\ket{v}$. The product of bra and ket is called a \emph{bra-ket}; it is the inner product of the last lecture:
\begin{equation}
	\bra{v} = (\ket{v})\adj = \begin{pmatrix} v_1^* & \cdots & v_n^* \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
	\braket{v}{w} = (\ket{v})\adj\ket{w} = \vb{v}\adj\vb{w}.
\end{equation}
This is not a new definition but a new notation for the same objects.

\begin{historical}
	The notation is due to Paul Dirac. The word \emph{bracket} is split in two: on the left stands the ``bra'', on the right the ``ket'', and the ``c'' has gone missing somewhere.
\end{historical}

The notation shows its advantage with a rule for linear combinations. For complex numbers $\alpha,\beta$,
\begin{equation}
	(\alpha\ket{v} + \beta\ket{w})\adj = \alpha^*\bra{v} + \beta^*\bra{w},
\end{equation}
because the dagger operation conjugates every entry. With it we compute inner products without writing the vectors as columns. In infinitely many dimensions a vector does not fit into a finite column, and there we compute exactly like this.

\begin{example}
	Let $\ket{v} = \ket{e_0} + i\ket{e_1}$ and $\ket{w} = \ket{e_0} + \ket{e_1}$. By the rule, $\bra{v} = \bra{e_0} - i\bra{e_1}$; note the minus sign. With $\braket{e_0}{e_0} = \braket{e_1}{e_1} = 1$ and $\braket{e_0}{e_1} = \braket{e_1}{e_0} = 0$,
	\begin{equation}
		\begin{split}
			\braket{v}{w} &= (\bra{e_0} - i\bra{e_1})(\ket{e_0} + \ket{e_1}) \\
			&= \braket{e_0}{e_0} + \braket{e_0}{e_1} - i\braket{e_1}{e_0} - i\braket{e_1}{e_1} = 1 - i.
		\end{split}
	\end{equation}
	Computed with columns, the same: $1\cdot 1 + (-i)\cdot 1 = 1 - i$.
\end{example}

Let us be honest: in two dimensions the first route is more work. In higher dimensions, however, you will very quickly discover a love for the ket notation.

\subsection{Inner products}
On $\mathbb{C}^n$ there are other inner products besides $\vb{v}\adj\vb{w}$, infinitely many in fact; the following definition covers them all. In calculations with columns we keep using $\vb{v}\adj\vb{w}$. We need an inner product because quantum mechanics is probabilistic: it is our tool for probabilities.

\begin{definition}
	An \emph{inner product} on a complex vector space $V$ assigns to any two vectors $\ket{\phi},\ket{\psi}\in V$ a complex number $\braket{\phi}{\psi}$. It is additive in each argument, $\braket{\phi_1 + \phi_2}{\psi} = \braket{\phi_1}{\psi} + \braket{\phi_2}{\psi}$ and likewise in the second, and for all $z\in\mathbb{C}$
	\begin{equation}
		\braket{\phi}{z\psi} = z\braket{\phi}{\psi}, \qquad \braket{z\phi}{\psi} = z^*\braket{\phi}{\psi}.
	\end{equation}
	In addition there is \emph{positivity}: $\braket{\psi}{\psi}$ is real and $\geq 0$, and zero only for $\ket{\psi} = 0$.
\end{definition}

The inner product $\vb{v}\adj\vb{w}$ satisfies all three conditions. The star in the first argument matters: if the inner product were linear there, then $\braket{i\psi}{i\psi} = -\braket{\psi}{\psi}$, and both sides could be nonnegative only for $\braket{\psi}{\psi} = 0$, even when $\ket{\psi} \neq 0$.

Positivity allows us to define the length of a vector, the \emph{norm}
\begin{equation}
	\norm{\psi} = \sqrt{\braket{\psi}{\psi}} \geq 0.
\end{equation}
With the inner product $\vb{v}\adj\vb{w}$, $\norm{v}^2 = \vb{v}\adj\vb{v} = \sum_j\abs{v_j}^2$.

From the three conditions follow conjugate symmetry and the \emph{Schwarz inequality}, and from the latter the triangle inequality for finitely many vectors:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{gathered}
		\braket{\phi}{\psi} = \braket{\psi}{\phi}^*, \qquad
		\abs{\braket{\phi}{\psi}} \leq \norm{\phi}\,\norm{\psi}, \\
		\norm{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\ket{\psi_j}} \leq \sum_{j=1}^{N}\norm{\psi_j}.
	\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
Both inequalities are working tools. The Schwarz inequality lies behind Heisenberg's uncertainty relation (Lecture~9); both are proved with the same step: the inner product of a cleverly chosen vector with itself is never negative. With the triangle inequality we bound quantities.

Two vectors are called \emph{orthogonal}, $\ket{\phi} \perp \ket{\psi}$, if $\braket{\phi}{\psi} = 0$. Caution: this does \emph{not} mean that one of the vectors is zero; positivity forces that only for $\ket{\phi} = \ket{\psi}$. A system of vectors $\ket{\phi_j}$ with $j$ from an index set $X$ is called \emph{orthonormal} if
\begin{equation}
	\braket{\phi_j}{\phi_k} = \delta_{jk} \quad \text{for all } j,k\in X.
\end{equation}
Here $\delta_{jk}$ is the Kronecker delta, $1$ for $j = k$ and $0$ otherwise; it is not the Dirac delta function, which comes later.

\subsection{Hilbert space}
Now we have enough together to define Hilbert spaces.

\begin{definition}
	A \emph{Hilbert space} $\Hil$ is a complex vector space with inner product that is complete with respect to the norm $\norm{\psi}^2 = \braket{\psi}{\psi}$. \emph{Complete} means: if the members of a sequence $\ket{\psi_1},\ket{\psi_2},\dots$ in $\Hil$ come arbitrarily close to one another, $\norm{\psi_m - \psi_k} \to 0$ as $m,k \to \infty$, then the sequence has a limit $\ket{\psi}\in\Hil$, that is $\norm{\psi_m - \psi} \to 0$.
\end{definition}

Completeness is important but subtle; it has to do with convergence in infinite-dimensional spaces. In $\mathbb{C}^n$ with $\vb{v}\adj\vb{w}$ it holds automatically, and for calculations there a Hilbert space is simply a complex vector space with inner product; for the qubit this is $\mathbb{C}^2$. In infinitely many dimensions this shortcut does not hold: there one needs completeness, for instance for the expansion in a basis with infinitely many vectors.

\subsection{Bases}
We need bases for a practical reason: most calculations in quantum mechanics only become explicit once a basis has been chosen. Here we consider only finite index sets $X$. An orthonormal system $\{\ket{\phi_j}\}_{j\in X}$ in $\Hil$ is called a \emph{basis} (more precisely an \emph{orthonormal basis}) of $\Hil$ if it is \emph{complete}:
\begin{equation}
	\norm{\psi}^2 = \sum_{j\in X}\abs{\braket{\phi_j}{\psi}}^2 \quad \text{for all } \ket{\psi}\in\Hil.
\end{equation}
Confusingly, one says complete here as well, but means something different from the completeness of the Hilbert space.

The pairs $\ket{e_0},\ket{e_1}$ and $\ket{e_+},\ket{e_-}$ are bases of $\mathbb{C}^2$. The second pair is orthonormal too: $\braket{e_+}{e_-} = \tfrac12(1 - 1) = 0$ and $\norm{e_\pm}^2 = \tfrac12(1 + 1) = 1$. For $\ket{\psi} = \alpha\ket{e_0} + \beta\ket{e_1}$ we have $\norm{\psi}^2 = \abs{\alpha}^2 + \abs{\beta}^2$; the right-hand side gives the same, with $\braket{e_0}{\psi} = \alpha$ and $\braket{e_1}{\psi} = \beta$ as well as with $\braket{e_\pm}{\psi} = (\alpha \pm \beta)/\sqrt{2}$, because $\abs{\alpha + \beta}^2 + \abs{\alpha - \beta}^2 = 2(\abs{\alpha}^2 + \abs{\beta}^2)$. In $\mathbb{C}^n$ with $\vb{v}\adj\vb{w}$ there is always a basis, the columns with a $1$ in the $j$-th entry and zeros everywhere else. In the infinite-dimensional case everything is harder: that a basis exists has to be proved, and the proof is hard. Bases with infinitely many vectors are treated in the web course.

The most important property of a basis is that every vector $\ket{\Psi}\in\Hil$ can be expanded in it, with the coefficients $c_j = \braket{\phi_j}{\Psi}$:
\begin{equation}
	\eqbox{\ket{\Psi} = \sum_{j\in X}\braket{\phi_j}{\Psi}\,\ket{\phi_j}.}
\end{equation}
For with $\ket{\xi} = \ket{\Psi} - \sum_j c_j\ket{\phi_j}$, orthonormality gives $\braket{\xi}{\xi} = \norm{\Psi}^2 - \sum_j\abs{c_j}^2$, which is zero by completeness, and by positivity $\ket{\xi} = 0$. This equation is one form of the so-called \emph{completeness relation}: every vector is the sum of its components along the basis vectors, and the coefficients are inner products. It looks harmless but is used all the time.

How does one build bases in practice? With the orthonormalisation procedure of Gram and Schmidt. From an arbitrary list $\ket{\Phi_1},\dots,\ket{\Phi_N}$ one determines one after another
\begin{equation}
	\ket{\Psi_n} = \ket{\Phi_n} - \sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{\braket{\Psi_k}{\Phi_n}}{\norm{\Psi_k}^2}\,\ket{\Psi_k},
\end{equation}
where terms with $\ket{\Psi_k} = 0$ are left out, because otherwise we would divide by zero. Then $\braket{\Psi_k}{\Psi_n} = 0$ for $k<n$, and the vectors $\ket{\Psi_n}/\norm{\Psi_n}$ with $\ket{\Psi_n} \neq 0$ always form an orthonormal system. If no vector other than the zero vector is orthogonal to all the $\ket{\Phi_n}$, this system is a basis of $\Hil$.

\begin{example}
	In $\mathbb{C}^2$ we start with two made-up vectors,
	\begin{equation}
		\ket{\Phi_1} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
		\ket{\Phi_2} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}.
	\end{equation}
	In the first step the sum runs from $1$ to $0$; nothing is summed, and $\ket{\Psi_1} = \ket{\Phi_1}$. In the second step, with $\braket{\Psi_1}{\Phi_2} = 2$ and $\norm{\Psi_1}^2 = 4$,
	\begin{equation}
		\ket{\Psi_2} = \ket{\Phi_2} - \frac{\braket{\Psi_1}{\Phi_2}}{\norm{\Psi_1}^2}\,\ket{\Psi_1}
		= \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} - \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}
		= \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}.
	\end{equation}
	Divided by the norms $\norm{\Psi_1} = 2$ and $\norm{\Psi_2} = 1$, the made-up list becomes the basis $\ket{e_0},\ket{e_1}$. The condition holds, because $\braket{\Phi_1}{\psi} = 2\psi_1 = 0$ and $\braket{\Phi_2}{\psi} = \psi_1 + \psi_2 = 0$ imply $\ket{\psi} = 0$.
\end{example}
