\section{Lecture: The Postulates of Quantum Mechanics}
The numbers that quantum mechanics delivers as results are probabilities or, somewhat more generally, expectation values. That the theory delivers only probabilities at all is the real break with classical physics. Classically a probability is always an expression of missing information; quantum mechanically it is fundamental, and there are measurements that cannot be carried out jointly with full precision.

One compares a probability with experiment by repeating the experiment sufficiently often and forming the frequency of hits; every run carried out by the same \emph{procedure} counts as a repetition, not necessarily with the same apparatus. In every experiment one distinguishes the \emph{preparation} and the \emph{measurement}. The theory must therefore contain at least: quantities for the preparations, quantities for the measurements, and a formula that assigns a probability to every combination. For an arbitrary probabilistic theory (a GPT, a general probabilistic theory) the web course introduces these three ingredients in Chapter~2; quantum theory is such a theory, built from a Hilbert space.

\subsection{The Postulates}

\begin{greybox}[On the status of the postulates]
You often hear that we do not understand quantum mechanics. That misses the point: we have mastered the formalism, and for over fifty years we have had postulates that give us the best models of the universe. What we cannot do is \emph{justify} them any further; they are given to us by nature.
\end{greybox}

We set up the postulates step by step, each more specialised than the one before. You know the first, P0, from Lecture~1: Physical theories are probabilistic theories: predictions in the form of probabilities.

\begin{merksatz}[P1]
	To every quantum mechanical system we assign a Hilbert space $\Hil$ (this is morally part of the definition of the state space for a GPT).
\end{merksatz}
For a \emph{qubit} $\Hil = \mathbb{C}^2$, for a \emph{qudit} $\Hil = \mathbb{C}^d$, for a particle in space the space $L^2(\mathbb{R}^3)$ of square-integrable functions. Which Hilbert space belongs to a system is determined by experiment, not by the theory. P1 speaks of \emph{one} system; the Hilbert space of several systems is built later with a further recipe, the tensor product. In this lecture $\Hil$ is finite-dimensional.

\begin{merksatz}[P2]
	State space = positive linear functionals on $\BdOp{\Hil}$ with norm 1. In the trace-class case these are density operators. Extreme points $\Rightarrow$ pure states.
\end{merksatz}
Here $\BdOp{\Hil}$ is the set of operators on $\Hil$. A state thus assigns a number linearly to every operator: to every Hermitian quantity its expectation value, to positive operators a non-negative number and to the identity operator the number one; ``norm~1'' here means precisely: the value on $\mathbb{I}$ is $1$. In finite dimension every such assignment has the form $\op{A}\mapsto\tr(\rho\,\op{A})$ with exactly one operator $\rho$; the ``trace-class case'' needs attention only in infinitely many dimensions. One sets $\melem{y}{\rho}{x}$ equal to the value on $\ketbra{x}{y}$ (the indices chosen so that $\tr(\rho\,\op{A})$ comes out) and uses $\op{A} = \sum_{x,y}\melem{x}{\op{A}}{y}\ketbra{x}{y}$; the rest of the proof is in the web course.
\begin{definition}
	A \emph{density operator} (state, density matrix) is a positive operator $\rho$ on $\Hil$ with $\tr(\rho) = 1$.
\end{definition}
A warning is in order here: the Hilbert space does \emph{not} directly describe the states. Many books say the states are the wave functions; that is not quite right. The Hilbert space is the space \emph{in which} we define states; the states themselves are operators. A system that interacts with its environment is in general not in a pure state, and completely isolated systems do not exist in physics: the electromagnetic field always couples in. Mixed states are therefore the normal case.

Mixtures $\lambda\rho_1 + (1-\lambda)\rho_2$ with $0\leq\lambda\leq 1$ are again density operators. With the spectral decomposition every density operator is a mixture $\rho = \sum_x p_x\ketbra{\phi_x}{\phi_x}$ with $p_x\geq 0$ and $\sum_x p_x = 1$. The \emph{pure} states, the extreme points, are the states that are not proper mixtures; these are exactly the $\rho = \ketbra{\psi}{\psi}$, so they are given by a vector $\ket{\psi}$, its \emph{wave function} (the converse is proved in the web course). $\ket{\psi}$ and $e^{i\alpha}\ket{\psi}$ give the same state. Because $\tr(\ketbra{\psi}{\psi}) = \braket{\psi}{\psi}$ (Lecture~5), only normalised vectors represent pure states. One also calls $\ketbra{\psi}{\psi}$ a one-dimensional projector; ``one-dimensional'' refers to no spatial dimension, but to its image being one-dimensional.

\begin{example}[States of a qubit]
For $\Hil = \mathbb{C}^2$, $\rho$ is a positive $2\times 2$ matrix with trace one. Positivity requires $\rho_{00} = p\geq 0$, the trace condition forces $\rho_{11} = 1-p$, and because $\rho_{11}\geq 0$ as well, $p$ lies between zero and one. Hermiticity makes the off-diagonal entries complex conjugates of each other:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qubitzustand}
	\rho = \begin{pmatrix} p & x \\ x^* & 1-p\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
This does not yet exhaust positivity; it requires that \emph{both} eigenvalues be non-negative; because their sum is $1$, that means: their product is non-negative, so
\begin{equation}
	\det\rho = p(1-p) - \abs{x}^2 \geq 0, \qquad \text{i.\,e.} \qquad \bigl(p-\tfrac12\bigr)^2 + \abs{x}^2 \leq \tfrac14.
\end{equation}
A qubit state thus depends on three real numbers, and the triples $(p, \real x, \imag x)$ lie in a ball of radius $\tfrac12$ about $(\tfrac12, 0, 0)$, the \emph{maximally mixed} state $\mathbb{I}/2$; the pure states lie on its boundary.
\end{example}

\begin{exercise}
Compute the two eigenvalues of the matrix in Eq.~(\ref{eq:qubitzustand}) and derive from them the condition $p(1-p) - \abs{x}^2 \ge 0$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{merksatz}[P3]
	Detectors = dual of state space = operators $\op{F}$ with $0 \leq \op{F} \leq \mathbb{I}$.
\end{merksatz}
A detector is a measuring apparatus with the possible responses ``yes'' (a hit) and ``no''. For operators, $\op{A}\leq\op{B}$ means that $\op{B}-\op{A}$ is positive. So P3 requires \emph{two} conditions, $\op{F}\geq 0$ \emph{and} $\mathbb{I}-\op{F}\geq 0$, and on the right stands the identity operator, not the number one. ``Dual of state space'' means: the functions $\rho\mapsto\tr(\rho\,\op{F})$ are exactly the functions $f$ on the density operators with values between $0$ and $1$ that respect mixtures, $f(\lambda\rho_1 + (1-\lambda)\rho_2) = \lambda f(\rho_1) + (1-\lambda)f(\rho_2)$.

\begin{merksatz}[P4]
	Born rule = probability of detection for a state prepared as density operator $\rho$ is $\tr(\rho\,\op{F})$.
\end{merksatz}
For a pure state this is not a further assumption but a calculation with the cyclicity of the trace of Lecture~5; for a projector $\op{F} = \ketbra{\phi}{\phi}$ it becomes the form found in every textbook:
\begin{equation}
	\tr\bigl(\ketbra{\psi}{\psi}\op{F}\bigr) = \tr\bigl(\bra{\psi}\op{F}\ket{\psi}\bigr) = \melem{\psi}{\op{F}}{\psi}, \qquad \melem{\psi}{\op{F}}{\psi} = \abs{\braket{\phi}{\psi}}^2.
\end{equation}
Make it a habit at the start to say explicitly, in every calculation, which postulate you are using.

\begin{example}[The Z measurement on a qubit]
The standard measurement on today's quantum computers is the Z measurement, named after $\pauli{z} = \ketbra{0}{0} - \ketbra{1}{1}$, with the detector $\op{F} = \ketbra{0}{0} = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$. Both conditions have to be checked: $\op{F}$ is a projector, hence positive, and separately $\mathbb{I}-\op{F} = \ketbra{1}{1}$ is a projector as well. Only now may we call $\op{F}$ a detector. With the state~(\ref{eq:qubitzustand}),
\begin{equation}
	\rho\,\op{F} = \begin{pmatrix} p & 0 \\ x^* & 0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \tr(\rho\,\op{F}) = p:
\end{equation}
with probability $p$ we get a hit. This justifies reading the diagonal entries of a density matrix as probabilities, namely for the measurement in the basis in which one writes the matrix down.
\end{example}

A measuring apparatus often delivers one of several outcomes $x$ from a set $X$; $x$ is now an outcome, not a basis index. For every outcome one asks whether it has occurred; that gives detectors $\op{F}_x$ with
\begin{equation}
	\op{F}_x \geq 0, \qquad \sum_{x\in X}\op{F}_x = \mathbb{I},
\end{equation}
a \emph{measurement} (a POVM, \emph{positive operator-valued measure}). By P4, $x\mapsto\tr(\rho\,\op{F}_x)$ is a probability distribution. A detector that reports the state $\ket{0}$ only with probability $q$, $0 < q < 1$, and never responds to $\ket{1}$, has for example $\op{F} = q\ketbra{0}{0}$; that is not a projector. If, on the other hand, every $\op{F}_x$ is a projector, the measurement is called \emph{projection-valued}. If the outcomes are real numbers, their expectation value is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:observableA}
	\sum_x x\,\tr(\rho\,\op{F}_x) = \tr(\rho\,\op{A}), \qquad \op{A} = \sum_x x\,\op{F}_x,
\end{equation}
and $\op{A}$ is Hermitian. In the projection-valued case, conversely, the measurement is already determined by $\op{A}$: the $\op{F}_x$ are the projectors onto the eigenspaces of $\op{A}$, and by the spectral theorem every Hermitian operator thus has a projection-valued measurement. One therefore often reads as a definition that the observables are exactly the Hermitian operators. That is the projection-valued special case; the detector with $\op{F} = q\ketbra{0}{0}$ is not contained in it.

\subsection{Time Evolution}
A state characterises not a single system but a preparation procedure that can be set going at any time. For a closed system whose conditions do not change with time, only the \emph{relative} time delay between preparation and measurement counts; that is an assumption about the system, not a consequence of reproducibility.

\begin{merksatz}[P5]
	That is it; dynamics are symmetries. But let us add, as in the notes, the specification of the Heisenberg/Schroedinger dynamics.
\end{merksatz}
``Dynamics are symmetries'' means: time evolutions are symmetries of the state space, as we shall see in a moment. An experiment with a delayed measurement is simply an experiment with a different measurement. The rule for it is itself a postulate, not provable from P0 to P4; for the reversible dynamics of a closed system it reads: every kind of system is further characterised by a Hermitian \emph{Hamiltonian} $\op{\Ham}$, which determines the \emph{time-evolution operator} $\op{U}_t = e^{-\frac{it}{\hbar}\op{\Ham}}$ of Lecture~5. If one applies the measurement with a delay $t$, one obtains
\begin{equation}
	\tr\bigl(\op{U}_t\rho\,\op{U}_t\adj\op{F}\bigr) = \tr\bigl(\rho\,\op{U}_t\adj\op{F}\op{U}_t\bigr).
\end{equation}
The equation follows from the cyclicity of the trace. On the left it is natural to introduce $\rho_t = \op{U}_t\rho\,\op{U}_t\adj$ as a time-dependent state, with fixed measurements; that is the \emph{Schrödinger picture}, for pure states $\ket{\psi_t} = \op{U}_t\ket{\psi}$. On the right it is natural to introduce time-dependent observables $\op{A}_t = \op{U}_t\adj\op{A}\op{U}_t$ with a fixed state; that is the \emph{Heisenberg picture}. Both pictures give the same probabilities.

That $\op{\Ham}$ is Hermitian is essential: it guarantees that $\op{U}_t$ is unitary for all $t$, and for unitary $\op{U}$, $\op{U}\rho\,\op{U}\adj$ is again a density operator and $0\leq\op{U}\adj\op{F}\op{U}\leq\mathbb{I}$ again a detector: $\melem{\psi}{\op{U}\rho\,\op{U}\adj}{\psi} = \melem{\op{U}\adj\psi}{\rho}{\op{U}\adj\psi}\geq 0$, $\tr(\op{U}\rho\,\op{U}\adj) = \tr(\rho\,\op{U}\adj\op{U}) = 1$, and the same calculation with $\op{F}$ and with $\mathbb{I}-\op{F}$ gives the two inequalities. $\op{U}_t$ maps the state space reversibly onto itself and preserves mixtures and pure states; in this sense dynamics is a symmetry. $\op{\Ham}$ is \emph{additional} information about the kind of system. For unbounded Hamiltonians, which occur in infinitely many dimensions, it has to say ``self-adjoint'' instead of ``Hermitian''; the web course treats this.

\begin{example}[A qubit under the Z measurement]
With $\op{\Ham} = \varepsilon\pauli{x}$ and $\op{U}_t = \cos(\varepsilon t/\hbar)\mathbb{I} - i\sin(\varepsilon t/\hbar)\pauli{x}$ from Lecture~5, the Z measurement in the state $\ketbra{0}{0}$ gives after a time $t$
\begin{equation}
	\tr\bigl(\rho_t\ketbra{0}{0}\bigr) = \abs{\melem{0}{\op{U}_t}{0}}^2 = \cos^2\Bigl(\frac{\varepsilon t}{\hbar}\Bigr).
\end{equation}
A state~(\ref{eq:qubitzustand}) with $\real x = 0$ stays in this plane: with $p-\tfrac12 = r\cos\varphi$ and $\imag x = r\sin\varphi$, $p_t - \tfrac12 = r\cos(\varphi + 2\varepsilon t/\hbar)$ and $\imag x_t = r\sin(\varphi + 2\varepsilon t/\hbar)$. The factor $2$ is the same as in $\cos^2\theta = \tfrac12(1 + \cos 2\theta)$: for $\ketbra{0}{0}$, $r = \tfrac12$ and $\varphi = 0$.
\end{example}

A significant gap remains in these postulates: nowhere is it said which operators $\rho$ or $\op{F}$ one should assign to a given experiment. That cannot be settled by a few general postulates. The most common approach is to single out standard observables, as a rule by their behaviour under the symmetries of space and time: position, momentum, energy, angular momentum. Whoever wants a more precise assignment has to analyse the measurement process itself. P0 to P5 do not say what happens to the system after a measurement either; we do not treat repeated measurements on the same system here. For almost all questions of this lecture we get by with the standard observables.
