The hydrogen atom consists of a proton and an electron. A first crude model of the atom is obtained by approximating the proton as an infinitely massive point mass. With this approximation the hydrogen atom can be modelled as a single particle (the electron) moving in the central potential 
\begin{equation}
	V(r) = -\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi \epsilon_0 r}
\end{equation}
of the proton.

The electron here is not a point on an orbit but a cloud; the proton likewise,
though the difference in mass lets it pass as a point mass. The reduction to one
particle is not an approximation: relative coordinates and the reduced mass
$\mu = m_{\text{e}}m_{\text{p}}/(m_{\text{e}}+m_{\text{p}})$ make it exact; the
approximation enters only with $\mu \approx m_{\text{e}}$. Calculations of this
kind determined the hydrogen lines, and those lines were used to measure the
distances to distant galaxies.

The radial equation is a one-dimensional problem in the effective potential
$U_\ell(r)$ shown in the figure above. It can be solved with a power series ansatz,
the Frobenius method; that is so laborious that we shall simply write the solutions
down and check them by substitution.

The solutions of the radial equation 
\begin{equation}
	\left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_{\text{e}}}\frac{d^2}{dr^2} + \frac{\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)}{2m_{\text{e}}r^2} + V(r) - E\right)u(r) = 0.
\end{equation}
are of the form
\begin{equation}
	R_{n\ell}(r) = \sqrt{\frac{(n-\ell-1)!(2\kappa)^3}{2n(n+\ell)!}}(2\kappa r)^\ell e^{-\kappa r}\text{L}_{n-\ell-1}^{2\ell +1}(2\kappa r),	
\end{equation}
where 
\begin{equation}
	\kappa = \frac{\sqrt{2m_{\text{e}}|E|}}{\hbar} = \frac{Z}{n a_0}
\end{equation}
and the $L_r^s$ are the Laguerre polynomials. The associated eigenvalues read
\begin{equation}
	\eqbox{E_n = -\frac{(Ze)^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{1}{a_0}\frac{1}{2n^2},}
\end{equation}
with \emph{principal quantum number} $n=1,2,\ldots$ and \emph{Bohr radius} 
\begin{equation}
	a_0 = \frac{4\pi\epsilon_0\hbar^2}{m_{\text{e}}e^2} = 5.29177210903(80) \times 10^{-11} \text{m}.
\end{equation}
The counting starts at $n=1$; there is no $n=0$. In the term scheme the
infinitely many levels pile up at the ionisation threshold $E=0$.
\begin{merksatz}
It is not the eigenvalues that are observable, but their differences: in a
transition $n \to n'$ with $n'<n$ the electron emits a photon of energy
$E_n-E_{n'}$, and these are the spectral lines.
\end{merksatz}

It is not the levels that are coloured here, but the transitions: the length of
each arrow is the photon energy, drawn to scale, and the Lyman arrows are about four
times as long as the Balmer arrows. On the right the pile-up at $E=0$ returns as the

The complete eigenfunctions read
\begin{equation}
	\psi_{n\ell m}(r,\theta,\phi) = R_{n\ell}(r)\text{Y}_{\ell}^m(\theta,\phi).
\end{equation}
For each $n$ the possible angular momentum quantum numbers are $\ell = 0,1,2, \ldots, n-1$ ($\equiv \text{s}, \text{p}, \text{d}, \text{f}, \ldots $). For each $\ell$ there are $2\ell +1$ possible values of the magnetic quantum number $m = -\ell, -\ell+1, \ldots, \ell$. One important remark: the energy is independent of $m$ and $\ell$. 

The first few Laguerre polynomials read
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		L_0^s(x) &= 1 \\
		L_1^s(x) &= -x+(s+1) \\
		L_2^s(x) &= \frac{x^2}{2}-(s+2)x + \frac{1}{2}(s+1)(s+2) \\
		&\vdots
	\end{split}
\end{equation}
We therefore obtain the following eigenfunctions $\psi_{n\ell m}(r,\theta,\phi)$ up to $n=2$:
\begin{align*}
	\psi_{100}(r,\theta,\phi) &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\left(\frac{Z}{a_0}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}e^{-\frac{Zr}{a_0}} &\quad & 1\text{s} \\
	\psi_{200}(r,\theta,\phi) &= \frac{1}{4\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(\frac{Z}{a_0}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\left(2-\frac{Zr}{a_0}\right)e^{-\frac{Zr}{2a_0}} &\quad & 2\text{s} \\
	\psi_{210}(r,\theta,\phi) &= \frac{1}{4\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(\frac{Z}{a_0}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\frac{Zr}{a_0}e^{-\frac{Zr}{2a_0}}\cos(\theta) &\quad & 2\text{p} \quad (m=0) \\
	\psi_{21\pm1}(r,\theta,\phi) &= \mp\frac{1}{8\sqrt{\pi}}\left(\frac{Z}{a_0}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\frac{Zr}{a_0}e^{-\frac{Zr}{2a_0}}\sin(\theta)e^{\pm i\phi} &\quad & 2\text{p} \quad (m=\pm1). \\
\end{align*}

The usual colourful orbital pictures show only $|\psi_{n\ell m}|^2$, not the
phase.
