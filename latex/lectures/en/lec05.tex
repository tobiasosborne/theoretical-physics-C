\section{Lecture: Trace, Diagonalisation, Functional Calculus}

With this lecture the mathematical toolkit of quantum mechanics is complete. The position basis $\ket{x}$, with which this lecture used to begin, comes with the particle on a line in Lecture~7.

\subsection{Trace}
From matrix algebra you know the sum of the diagonal entries. It comes up in quantum mechanics all the time.
\begin{definition}
	Let $\{\ket{x}\}_{x\in X}$ be an orthonormal basis of $\Hil$ with a finite index set; here $x$ is just an index. The \emph{trace} of an operator $\op{A}$ is
	\begin{equation}
		\tr(\op{A}) = \sum_{x\in X} \melem{x}{\op{A}}{x}.
	\end{equation}
\end{definition}
The number does not depend on the orthonormal basis: inserting the completeness relation $\mathbb{I} = \sum_y \ketbra{y}{y}$ of a second basis gives $\sum_x \melem{x}{\op{A}}{x} = \sum_{x,y} \melem{y}{\op{A}}{x}\braket{x}{y} = \sum_y \melem{y}{\op{A}}{y}$; in the last step the sum over $x$ is the completeness relation of the first basis. The same calculation shows the rule we will use all the time. If $\op{A}\colon \Hil_1 \to \Hil_2$ and $\op{B}\colon \Hil_2 \to \Hil_1$ are linear, then
\begin{equation}
	\tr_2(\op{A}\op{B}) = \sum_{x,y} \melem{y}{\op{A}}{x}\melem{x}{\op{B}}{y} = \tr_1(\op{B}\op{A}),
\end{equation}
with orthonormal bases $\{\ket{x}\}$ of $\Hil_1$ and $\{\ket{y}\}$ of $\Hil_2$; the index on the trace says in which space it is taken. So under the trace one may bring the last factor to the front; exchanging factors arbitrarily is not allowed. The most important case comes from the ket and the bra as maps $\ket{\Psi}\colon\mathbb{C}\to\Hil$ and $\bra{\Phi}\colon\Hil\to\mathbb{C}$ of Lecture~4. With the basis $\{1\}$ of $\mathbb{C}$ the trace of multiplication by a number is that number, so
\begin{equation}
	\tr\bigl(\ketbra{\Psi}{\Phi}\bigr) = \braket{\Phi}{\Psi}.
\end{equation}
The trace of a ket-bra is a bra-ket. For example, the projector $\op{B}$ of Lecture~4 equals $\ketbra{u}{u}$ with $\ket{u} = (1,1,1)/\sqrt{3}$, so $\tr(\op{B}) = \braket{u}{u} = 1$. In infinitely many dimensions the sum is a series that need not converge; there not every operator has a trace.

\subsection{Eigenvalues and diagonalisation}
General matrices are cumbersome to work with and do not commute with each other; diagonal operators, on the other hand, are as good as numbers. That is why we often have to \emph{diagonalise} operators. Making all operators diagonal at once is usually impossible; we always diagonalise one particular operator. We now make the notion of Lecture~4 precise. It needs no inner product.
\begin{definition}
	A vector $\ket{v}\neq 0$ is called an \emph{eigenvector} of $\op{A}$ with \emph{eigenvalue} $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}$ if $\op{A}\ket{v} = \lambda\ket{v}$. The eigenvectors with eigenvalue $\lambda$, together with the zero vector, form the \emph{eigenspace} for $\lambda$.

	$\op{A}$ is called \emph{diagonal} in the orthonormal basis $\{\ket{x}\}$ if $\melem{x}{\op{A}}{y} = \delta_{xy}\,a_x$, that is $\op{A} = \sum_x a_x\ketbra{x}{x}$, and \emph{unitarily diagonalisable} if there is an orthonormal basis in which $\op{A}$ is diagonal. We write $\diag(a_1,\dots,a_n)$ for the diagonal matrix.
\end{definition}
An eigenvector obtained from a system of equations almost never has norm $1$; one divides by its norm and has an eigenvector again. A diagonal operator multiplies each component by its own number, $\op{A}\ket{\Psi} = \sum_x a_x\braket{x}{\Psi}\ket{x}$; so every basis vector is an eigenvector. The converse holds as well, and so diagonalising and finding eigenvectors are the same task. Let $\{\ket{x}\}$ be a fixed orthonormal basis and $\{\ket{\phi_x}\}$ an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors with the same indices, $\op{A}\ket{\phi_x} = a_x\ket{\phi_x}$. Then
\begin{equation}
	\op{U} = \sum_x \ketbra{x}{\phi_x}
\end{equation}
is unitary, because with orthonormality and the completeness relation
\begin{equation}
	\op{U}\op{U}\adj = \sum_{x,y} \ket{x}\braket{\phi_x}{\phi_y}\bra{y} = \sum_x \ketbra{x}{x} = \mathbb{I},
\end{equation}
likewise $\op{U}\adj\op{U} = \mathbb{I}$ with $\op{U}\adj = \sum_x\ketbra{\phi_x}{x}$, and with $\melem{\phi_x}{\op{A}}{\phi_y} = a_y\,\delta_{xy}$ in the middle
\begin{equation}
	\op{U}\op{A}\op{U}\adj = \sum_x a_x \ketbra{x}{x}, \qquad
	\op{A} = \sum_x a_x \ketbra{\phi_x}{\phi_x}, \qquad
	\tr(\op{A}) = \sum_x a_x.
\end{equation}
The second equation is called the \emph{spectral decomposition}. Conversely, if $\op{U}$ is unitary and $\op{U}\op{A}\op{U}\adj$ is diagonal, the vectors $\op{U}\adj\ket{x}$ are an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors.

In linear algebra an operator is already called diagonalisable if it has some basis of eigenvectors; here we always mean the orthonormal, that is unitary, form. Not every operator can be diagonalised: the matrix with rows $(0,1)$ and $(0,0)$ has only the multiples of $(1,0)$ as eigenvectors. In quantum mechanics we are lucky: the Hermitian and the unitary operators can always be diagonalised in finite dimension. The first step is a trick one needs again and again: evaluate the same expression in two ways. Let $\op{A}$ be Hermitian with $\op{A}\ket{\phi} = a\ket{\phi}$ and $\op{A}\ket{\psi} = b\ket{\psi}$. Letting $\op{A}$ act to the right, $\melem{\phi}{\op{A}}{\psi} = b\braket{\phi}{\psi}$; letting it act to the left, it is $\braket{\op{A}\phi}{\psi} = a^*\braket{\phi}{\psi} = a\braket{\phi}{\psi}$, because $a\braket{\phi}{\phi} = \melem{\phi}{\op{A}}{\phi}$ is real (Lecture~4). Hence
\begin{equation}
	(a-b)\braket{\phi}{\psi} = 0:
\end{equation}
eigenvectors with different eigenvalues are orthogonal. That there are always enough of them, even when eigenvalues coincide, is what the following theorem says; its proof is in the web course.
\begin{merksatz}[Spectral theorem]
	On a finite-dimensional Hilbert space every Hermitian and every unitary operator has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, and so is unitarily diagonalisable.
\end{merksatz}
The finite dimension is essential: in infinitely many dimensions there are Hermitian operators entirely without eigenvectors; the web course shows an example.

\begin{example}
	Let $\Hil = \mathbb{C}^2$, a \emph{qubit}, with the basis $\ket{0},\ket{1}$, and $\pauli{x} = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$. From $\det(\pauli{x} - \lambda\mathbb{I}) = \lambda^2 - 1$ we obtain the eigenvalues $\pm 1$ with the normalised eigenvectors
	\begin{equation}
		\ket{\phi_0} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \ket{\phi_1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}.
	\end{equation}
	A ket is a column, a bra a row, so a ket-bra is a matrix:
	\begin{equation}
		\op{U} = \ketbra{0}{\phi_0} + \ketbra{1}{\phi_1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix},
	\end{equation}
	the \emph{Hadamard matrix}; one checks $\op{U}\pauli{x}\op{U}\adj = \diag(1,-1)$. If one exchanges the two eigenvectors, the same recipe gives a different unitary matrix, and the diagonal form becomes $\diag(-1,1)$: the diagonalisation is not unique.
\end{example}

\subsection{Functional calculus}
Because diagonal operators are as good as numbers, one can apply functions to them by applying the function to the numbers.
\begin{definition}
	Let $\op{A} = \sum_x a_x\ketbra{\phi_x}{\phi_x}$ be a spectral decomposition and $f$ a complex function on the eigenvalues. Then $f(\op{A}) = \sum_x f(a_x)\ketbra{\phi_x}{\phi_x}$.
\end{definition}
For a polynomial, $\op{A}^k = \sum_x a_x^k\ketbra{\phi_x}{\phi_x}$ shows that this agrees with substituting the operator into the polynomial; and because every function on the finitely many eigenvalues agrees with a polynomial (Lagrange interpolation, in the web course), $f(\op{A})$ does not depend on the chosen eigenbasis. The most important case: let $\op{\Ham} = \sum_x E_x\ketbra{\phi_x}{\phi_x}$ be Hermitian and let $t$ be real. In Lecture~6, $\op{\Ham}$ will be the Hamiltonian with energies $E_x$. Then
\begin{equation}
	\op{U}_t = e^{-\frac{it}{\hbar}\op{\Ham}} = \sum_x e^{-\frac{it}{\hbar}E_x}\ketbra{\phi_x}{\phi_x}.
\end{equation}
On every eigenvector $\op{U}_t$ acts as multiplication by a \emph{phase}. The chain is: the $E_x$ are real because $\op{\Ham}$ is Hermitian; therefore the phases have modulus $1$; therefore $\op{U}_t\adj\op{U}_t = \sum_x \abs{e^{-itE_x/\hbar}}^2\ketbra{\phi_x}{\phi_x} = \mathbb{I}$, and $\op{U}_t$ changes the norm of no vector. In finite dimension $\op{U}_t$ is also the sum of the exponential series $\sum_n \frac{1}{n!}\bigl(\frac{-it}{\hbar}\bigr)^n\op{\Ham}^n$, because every partial sum is a polynomial in $\op{\Ham}$, so on $\ket{\phi_x}$ it acts as the partial sum of the scalar series for $e^{-itE_x/\hbar}$, and these finitely many series converge.

\begin{example}
	Let $\op{\Ham} = \varepsilon\pauli{x}$ with an energy $\varepsilon>0$. With the eigenvalues $\pm\varepsilon$ and the eigenvectors from above,
	\begin{equation}
		\eqbox{\op{U}_t = e^{-i\varepsilon t/\hbar}\ketbra{\phi_0}{\phi_0} + e^{i\varepsilon t/\hbar}\ketbra{\phi_1}{\phi_1} = \cos\Bigl(\frac{\varepsilon t}{\hbar}\Bigr)\mathbb{I} - i\sin\Bigl(\frac{\varepsilon t}{\hbar}\Bigr)\pauli{x}}
	\end{equation}
	because $\mathbb{I} = \ketbra{\phi_0}{\phi_0} + \ketbra{\phi_1}{\phi_1}$ and $\pauli{x} = \ketbra{\phi_0}{\phi_0} - \ketbra{\phi_1}{\phi_1}$. The series gives the same: because $\pauli{x}^2 = \mathbb{I}$ it splits into the series for cosine and sine. This trick uses $\pauli{x}^2 = \mathbb{I}$; in general one calculates with the spectral decomposition.
\end{example}

\begin{merksatz}
	The operator $\op{U}_t$ describes the time evolution of a quantum system. So the solution of the Schrödinger equation is already here, before the equation itself has appeared. ``Solving the dynamics'' therefore means: diagonalising the Hamiltonian.
\end{merksatz}
