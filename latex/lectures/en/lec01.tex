\section{Lecture: The Birth of Quantum Mechanics}
We find ourselves in a quantum universe. Until 1900 we did not notice the quantum aspects of the universe, because classical physics supplied us with a very good approximation to the physics of everyday life. What stands in need of explanation is therefore not quantum mechanics, but the quality of the classical approximation. The keyword for that is \emph{decoherence}; it belongs to advanced quantum mechanics.

A remark on method, one that holds for the whole course: one can first work through the wrong, classical or semiclassical, models, in order to show afterwards that they do not work. That is not my style. From the very beginning we look at everything correctly; nowhere do we work through the Bohr model of the atom. This script contains only the core; derivations, deeper sections and interactive figures are in the web course.

\subsection{Why a new theory was needed}
The prehistory of quantum mechanics is a series of observations which gradually called into question the thesis that our universe is described by classical physics.
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \emph{Black-body radiation} (Planck, 1900). A black body reflects no incident radiation, yet at a temperature $T$ it radiates all the same. The spectrum of this radiation cannot be explained with classical physics. The right one follows from the assumption that light of frequency $\nu$ consists of \emph{light quanta} of energy $E = h\nu$. Planck originally postulated the constant $h$ as an ``auxiliary quantity'' in deriving the radiation law:
	\begin{equation*}
		u(\nu, T)\,\dd\nu = \frac{8\pi h \nu^3}{c^3}\,\frac{\dd\nu}{e^{h\nu/(\kB T)} - 1}
	\end{equation*}
	is the energy per volume in the frequency interval $\dd\nu$, with the speed of light $c$ and Boltzmann's constant $\kB$. We shall derive this law ourselves in the statistical part. Theoretical physics predominantly uses the \emph{reduced Planck constant}
	\begin{equation*}
		\eqbox{\hbar = \frac{h}{2\pi}}
	\end{equation*}
	\item \emph{Photoelectric effect} (Lenard; Einstein from 1905). Light can release electrons. For a fixed material their \emph{maximum} kinetic energy depends on the frequency of the light, not on its intensity. Classically that is strange: everywhere else more intensity means more energy. If a light quantum gives its energy to a single electron, the balance reads
	\begin{equation*}
		E_{\mathrm{kin,max}} = \tfrac12 m_{\mathrm{e}} v_{\max}^2 = h\nu - W
	\end{equation*}
	with the binding energy $W$ of the material (written non-relativistically). Below the frequency $W/h$ a single light quantum releases no electron in this model.
	\item \emph{Stability of atoms, line spectra, Franck-Hertz experiment}. By the Liénard-Wiechert potentials, every accelerated point charge radiates. An electron on an orbit around the nucleus would therefore lose energy all the time and fall into the nucleus, which it does not do. Line spectra and the Franck-Hertz experiment (1914) indicate that only certain, discrete energy levels are allowed in atoms.
	\item \emph{Compton effect}. How energy and momentum are shared out when light scatters off electrons is described correctly with light quanta of energy $h\nu$ and momentum $p = h/\lambda$, together with conservation of energy and momentum.
	\item \emph{Stern-Gerlach experiment} (1922). A beam of silver atoms passes through an inhomogeneous magnetic field. Classically it should fan out continuously; two separate beams are observed. For a fixed angular-momentum quantum number, the component of the angular momentum along the field takes only finitely many values. From such quantised angular momenta one can build qubits today.
	\item \emph{Interference of electrons}. The diffraction of electrons by crystals (Davisson and Germer, 1927) was an indication of wave properties of the electron, which de Broglie had postulated in 1924.
\end{enumerate}
I sum up the findings in two sentences.

\begin{merksatz}
Matter and radiation possess both wave and particle properties.
\end{merksatz}

\begin{merksatz}[The most important statement about quantum mechanics]
Quantum mechanics is a \emph{probabilistic theory}. It therefore gives us predictions only in the form of \emph{probabilities}.
\end{merksatz}

This second statement stands above the first: ``wave and particle properties'' remains a little vague, whereas the probabilistic statement is precise and underlies every quantum phenomenon. Classically the predictions are definite: the particle is at this place at this time. Probabilities enter there purely through missing information; there are no fundamental probabilities in classical physics. The statistical interpretation was supplied by Born in 1926, and for me it is the most important aspect of the theory. Please take this sentence away with you: in every oral examination I ask for the most important statement about quantum mechanics.

The interpretation of quantum mechanics is, despite many ideas, still disputed today; to this day there is no contradiction with it. Our assumption is that quantum mechanics is the correct description of the universe.

\subsection{What a physical theory supplies}
From the second statement I make a principle for every physical theory: physical theories are probabilistic theories, their predictions take the form of probabilities. I think of deterministic theories as a subset of probabilistic ones. A physical theory supplies data for preparations, dynamics and measurement.

\begin{greybox}[Every experiment in three steps]
To the theorist all experiments in physics look the same: something is \emph{prepared}, say a die or, in the laboratory, a beam of light; the experiment is \emph{run}; at the end we \emph{measure} and obtain one of the possible outcomes.
\input{figures/fig_vl01_experiment}
The first two steps can be combined into \emph{one} more complicated preparation: a loaded die is nothing other than an ordinary die to which something was done beforehand. Where one draws the line is a matter of taste; often preparation and measurement suffice.
\end{greybox}

The order of the material is fixed too: we go from the microscopic to the macroscopic, from the quantum mechanics of single systems via many particles to statistical mechanics and thermodynamics. At each stage one learns that every theory is an effective theory of the one below it, an approximation to the fundamental physics underneath.

\subsection{Probability for physicists}
The numbers that quantum mechanics delivers as its results are probabilities. Do not expect anything conceptually new: I take an intuitive understanding for granted; what matters is the \emph{notation} in which we shall later formulate quantum mechanics. We speak the language of physics: what mathematics calls a probability distribution is here a \emph{preparation} (state), and a random variable is a \emph{measurement}.

\subsubsection{Outcomes and preparations}
To model an experiment we need three things: the set of possible outcomes, the preparation and the measurement. To every system a set $X$ is assigned, the \emph{outcome set} (sample space); one obtains it by listing all possible outcomes. Subsets $S \subset X$ are called \emph{events}. For now let $X$ be finite. A \emph{preparation} is characterised by a probability $p(x)$ for every outcome $x$, a \emph{weight} with
\begin{equation}
	p(x) \geq 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \sum_{x \in X} p(x) = 1.
\end{equation}
The \emph{probability} of an event is $\mathbb{P}[S] = \sum_{x \in S} p(x)$.

\begin{example}
	For a die $X = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$, and ``the number shown is even'' is the event $S = \{2, 4, 6\}$. For a fair die $p(x) = 1/6$ for all $x$, so $\mathbb{P}[S] = 1/2$.
\end{example}

\begin{exercise}
	Describe the outcome set for \emph{two} dice. How many elements does it have?
\end{exercise}

For pairwise disjoint events $S_1, \dots, S_k$
\begin{equation}
	\mathbb{P}[S_1 \cup \dots \cup S_k] = \mathbb{P}[S_1] + \dots + \mathbb{P}[S_k].
\end{equation}
Disjointness is essential: if the events overlap, the right-hand side counts outcomes twice. In mathematics such a set function is called a \emph{measure}, and with $\mathbb{P}[X] = 1$ a \emph{probability measure}. We need no more measure theory than that.

\subsubsection{Measurements and expectation value}
A \emph{measurement} (observable, random variable) is a function $f\colon X \to \mathbb{R}$. The value $f(x)$ is the measured value when the outcome $x$ is certainly present. Note how generous this is: we impose no condition on a measurement, but two on a preparation. Preparation and measurement together give the \emph{expectation value}
\begin{equation}
	\eqbox{\mathbb{E}[f] = \sum_{x \in X} p(x) f(x)}
\end{equation}
It is linear, $\mathbb{E}[\alpha f + \gamma g] = \alpha\,\mathbb{E}[f] + \gamma\,\mathbb{E}[g]$, and for a constant $\mathbb{E}[c] = c$.

Where does the formula come from? One repeats the experiment $N$ times with the same preparation and obtains the outcomes $x_1, \dots, x_N$. If $x$ occurs $N(x)$ times among them, $h_N(x) = N(x)/N$ is the \emph{relative frequency}; it is itself a weight, and the mean of the series satisfies the exact identity
\begin{equation}
	\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} f(x_i) = \sum_{x \in X} h_N(x)\, f(x).
\end{equation}
With many independent repetitions $h_N(x)$ lies, with high probability, close to $p(x)$, and the mean of the series close to the expectation value. This reading is an interpretation: in a single series $h_N$ can deviate from $p$.

\subsubsection{Yes-no measurements}
The most important measurements take only the values $0$ and $1$. They are the model for detectors, such as a photon detector: click or no click. Quantum mechanics hinges on these two-valued measurements. Such a measurement is determined by the set $S = \{x \in X \colon f(x) = 1\}$; one writes
\begin{equation}
	\chi_S(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & x \in S, \\ 0, & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases}
\end{equation}
and calls $\chi_S$ the \emph{indicator function} of $S$. Hold on to this: indicator functions \emph{are} measurements, not a separate object alongside them. Their expectation value is the probability of their event:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:indicatorfunction}
	\mathbb{E}[\chi_S] = \sum_{x \in X} p(x)\, \chi_S(x) = \sum_{x \in S} p(x) = \mathbb{P}[S].
\end{equation}
Probabilities are therefore special expectation values. Conversely $\mathbb{P}$ fixes all expectation values, since $p(x) = \mathbb{P}[\{x\}]$.

\begin{exercise}
	Let $X = \{1, 2, 3\}$ and $p(x) = 1/3$ for all $x$. Compute $\mathbb{P}[S]$ for $S = \{1, 2\}$ and the expectation value of the measurement $f(x) = x^2$.
\end{exercise}

\subsubsection{Variance and determinism}
The expectation value says where the measured values lie on average; the \emph{variance} says how widely they scatter:
\begin{equation}
	\operatorname{Var}[f] = \mathbb{E}\bigl[(f - \mathbb{E}[f])^2\bigr] = \mathbb{E}[f^2] - \mathbb{E}[f]^2 .
\end{equation}
The second form follows from linearity. Written out, $\operatorname{Var}[f] = \sum_x p(x)\,(f(x) - \mathbb{E}[f])^2$ is a sum of numbers $\geq 0$. Hence $\operatorname{Var}[f] \geq 0$, and $\operatorname{Var}[f] = 0$ holds exactly when $f(x) = \mathbb{E}[f]$ for all $x$ with $p(x) > 0$. In quantum mechanics the variance plays a large role; we shall meet it again in the uncertainty relation.

\begin{example}
	For the fair die and the number shown, $f(x) = x$, we have $\mathbb{E}[f] = 21/6 = 7/2$; the expectation value need not be a possible measured value. With $\mathbb{E}[f^2] = 91/6$, $\operatorname{Var}[f] = 91/6 - 49/4 = 35/12$.
\end{example}

For a yes-no measurement $\chi_S^2 = \chi_S$, so
\begin{equation}
	\operatorname{Var}[\chi_S] = \mathbb{P}[S]\,\bigl(1 - \mathbb{P}[S]\bigr).
\end{equation}
It vanishes exactly when the answer is certain. If $p(a) = 1$ for an outcome $a$, then $\mathbb{E}[f] = f(a)$ for every measurement.

\begin{merksatz}[Determinism is a special case]
For a weight $p$ on a finite outcome set the following are equivalent: $p(a) = 1$ for an outcome $a$; every event has probability $0$ or $1$; every measurement has variance $0$. We call such preparations deterministic: the outcome of every measurement is fixed. This is how the definite predictions of classical physics fit into a probabilistic theory.
\end{merksatz}

\subsubsection{Continuous outcomes}
For quantum mechanics $X = \mathbb{R}$ is decisive: a particle in one dimension has a position, and the possible positions are the real numbers. We consider only preparations with a \emph{probability density}, a function $p$ on $\mathbb{R}$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ctsprob}
	\begin{aligned}
		p(x) &\geq 0, & \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} p(x)\,\dd x &= 1, \\
		\mathbb{P}[[a,b]] &= \int_a^b p(x)\,\dd x, & \mathbb{E}[f] &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} p(x) f(x)\,\dd x .
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $a \leq b$, and we use the expectation value only if $\int p(x) \abs{f(x)}\,\dd x < \infty$. Compared with the finite case, then, only the sum is replaced by an integral. In the literature one also finds $\int \mathbb{P}(\dd x)\, f(x)$; I do not much like this notation, you should only recognise it. Important: the value $p(x)$ of a density is not a probability. Probabilities are areas under the curve; a single point has probability $0$, and $p(x)$ may be larger than $1$.

\begin{example}
	The density
	\begin{equation}
		p(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-\frac12 x^2}
	\end{equation}
	is called the \emph{normal} or \emph{Gaussian distribution}; it is normalised. Is the particle to the left or to the right of the origin? Because $p(-x) = p(x)$, the areas to the left and to the right of the origin are equal, together $1$; so $\int_0^\infty p(x)\,\dd x = 1/2$. Be careful: the argument works \emph{only} for symmetric densities.
\end{example}

The expectation values of the coordinate function and its powers are called \emph{moments}, provided the integrals $\int \abs{x}^n p(x)\,\dd x$ are finite:
\begin{equation}
	M_n = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x^n\, p(x)\,\dd x .
\end{equation}
Only $M_0 = 1$, the normalisation, always exists; $M_1$, if it exists, is called the \emph{mean}. If $M_1$ and $M_2$ exist, the variance of the coordinate is, as in the finite case,
\begin{equation}
	\operatorname{Var}[\mathbb{P}] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} (x - M_1)^2\, p(x)\,\dd x = M_2 - M_1^2 .
\end{equation}
Mean and variance then carry the same information as $M_1$ and $M_2$. For the models considered here the language is now ready: preparations are weights or densities, measurements are functions, and the two together give expectation values when the integrals are finite.
